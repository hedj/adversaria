# Hold four, recall forty, recognise the rest

One person cannot learn 231 separate mechanisms and use them well. No single number answers the question, because three limits apply, each in its own unit. In one act, a person can combine about **4 interacting things**. Over months of daily use, a person keeps about **30–50 commands** in recall, and that figure held in Word, AutoCAD, GIMP and Unix whether the program offered 265 commands or about 2,000. Beyond that, people recognise roughly 100–135 more when a menu or a search puts them on screen. From these figures this report sets an estimated budget for a researcher's daily notebook: about **10–15 purpose-level concepts**, of which no more than **4–5** are needed before the first useful task. One regular grammar should express them, so that the user holds **no more than about 50 items** in all, and no act should combine more than 4. The earlier count of 231 is about 5 to 8 times the measured working vocabulary, and close to the 265 first-level functions of Word 97, of which the heaviest of 53 users used 119. To fit the budget, count by purpose, not by command. Strip interface and storage artefacts, merge redundant concepts, split overloaded ones, and put every concept on one shared substrate. Express operations as one verb–object grammar that follows the user's own categories, and leave the long tail to recognition. The evidence has firm limits. No study measured a personal knowledge tool. The vocabulary figures count commands, not concepts. The concept budget is derived, not measured. The case for composable grammars rests on 1980s command-language experiments reported without effect sizes, and on one study of 11 people.

*Labels used below. **Measured**: a count from logs, a survey or a lab study. **Self-report**: a survey or an interview, which the AutoCAD data show to be unreliable. **Argued**: a design analysis with no controlled measure. **Estimate**: this report's own derivation from measured figures. **Secondary**: a figure read in a source that cites the original.*

## Working memory caps each act near four things, not seven

The often-quoted "seven, plus or minus two" measures something other than what a designer needs. Miller's span of about seven items was measured with rehearsal and recoding allowed. Miller himself called absolute judgement and immediate memory "quite different kinds of limitations", and suspected that the shared seven was "only a pernicious, Pythagorean coincidence" ([Miller 1956](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Miller/)). With rehearsal blocked, adults hold **3 to 5 meaningful items, with model fits settling near 4**, and about 3 units when rehearsal is fully prevented, whether the units are single words or learnt pairs ([Cowan 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2864034/)). The Model Human Processor makes the same split: "pure" working memory of 3 chunks (range 2.5 to about 4.1) against an "effective" 7 (5–9) that borrows from long-term memory (secondary: [Human processor model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_processor_model)). Relating things is harder than holding them. Thirty academics interpreting statistical interactions lost accuracy and speed from three-way to four-way interactions, and **five-way interactions were at chance**. The authors concluded that "a structure defined on four variables is at the limit of human processing capacity" ([Halford et al. 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15660854/)). A user in mid-task also spends attention on the task, so the sober planning figure for new, unchunked things held at once is 3 to 4.

This limit binds what is *not* on the screen. It does not bind the length of a visible list. Choosing from a visible menu is recognition, not recall. Menu studies with 64 items favoured broad, shallow structures, with 8×8 giving the fastest acquisition and fewest errors among four layouts (secondary: [Hochheiser & Shneiderman 2000](https://www.cs.umd.edu/users/ben/papers/Hochheiser2000Performance.pdf)). Those results concern finding an item, and they cannot justify capping a feature set at seven or at 64. What the limit does bind is the current mode, a pending argument, the world a command acts in, its scope, and the path back. Cognitive load theory explains why the count of things to learn and their interaction are separate costs. Elements interact when they "must be assimilated simultaneously", and paired associates, such as a vocabulary, are "probably the ultimate in low element interactivity because the paired associates can be learned one at a time". A long list of them is slow to learn but does not overload working memory ([Sweller 1994](https://mrbartonmaths.com/resourcesnew/8.%20Research/Explicit%20Instruction/CLT,%20learning%20difficulty%20and%20instructional%20design.pdf)). Two budgets follow, and they must not be confused. A **vocabulary budget** counts independent operations learnt one at a time over weeks. An **interaction budget** counts what one act requires the user to combine, and it is paid on every use until the combination chunks. A command whose effect depends on a mode, a selection, a world and an argument sits at Halford's ceiling of four. Where that dependence is not essential to the user's task, it is extraneous load, which the designer controls and can remove ([Sweller 2010](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10648-010-9128-5)).

Modes are the commonest way an interface adds an interacting element without meaning to. In a vi-style editing task, **experts made slightly more mode errors than novices** (not significant). A mode held by keeping a foot pedal pressed cut errors more than a visual indicator did. The switching method explained 11–16 % of the variance in errors, and visual feedback 4–5 % ([Sellen, Kurtenbach & Buxton 1992](https://research.autodesk.com/app/uploads/2023/03/the-prevention-of-mode.pdf_recnGhCKDmVQoUNXq.pdf)). Raskin's rule, which is argued rather than measured, agrees with this evidence: eliminate modes, and turn any that must remain into quasimodes held by continuous action ([Raskin Center](https://www.raskincenter.org/rchi/core-principles)). Acme removes a mode by context instead: it has "no single notion of 'current directory'", and each command runs in the directory named by its own window's tag ([Pike 1994](https://9p.io/sys/doc/acme/acme.html)). A state the user must track in the head costs one interacting element in every act performed under it. A state that is held, or shown at the point of action, costs much less.

## Daily users settle on 30 to 50 commands whatever the program offers

Log studies of feature-rich programs give the best measured anchor for a vocabulary budget. The personal vocabulary stays about the same size across programs, while the share of the program it covers varies widely.

| Program | Commands offered | Per-user vocabulary | Shared core | Method |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Word 97 | 265 first-level functions | mean 40 used regularly, 72 ever used, 135 recognised; used range 8–119 | 12 used regularly by more than 75 % | self-report, 53 users ([McGrenere 2000](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/~joanna/papers/chi2000_poster_paper.pdf)) |
| AutoCAD | about 2,000 | largest group uses 31–40 | top 10 commands are 50 % of use; top 100 are 93 % | logs, 16,000 users, 6 months ([Matejka et al. 2009](https://research.autodesk.com/app/uploads/2023/03/communitycommands-command-recommendations-for.pdf_recZiyrKCEIuGQLZh.pdf)) |
| GIMP | about 500 | mean 34, median 27 (range 1–169); per session median 6, mean 9 | 15 used by more than half the users | logs, over 200 users, 2 years ([Lafreniere et al. 2009](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/research/tr/2009/CS-2009-27.pdf)) |
| Unix csh | 1,307 observed | mean 50 | under 3 % of commands shared by more than half | logs, 168 users (secondary, via [Lafreniere et al. 2009](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/research/tr/2009/CS-2009-27.pdf)) |

Three figures recur. A **shared core of about 12–15 commands** is used by most people. A **personal set of about 30–50** builds up over months. A single session uses **about 6–9**. The constancy of the personal set across programs of 265 to 2,000 commands suggests that the limit lies with the user and the task, not with the software. That cause is inferred, because the data are observational. Use is heavily skewed, and the long tail is personal: in GIMP, **257 of the 352 commands observed (73 %) were each used by no more than 10 % of users** ([Lafreniere et al. 2009](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/research/tr/2009/CS-2009-27.pdf)). Users also learn in a fairly fixed order that follows frequency of use, and they add commands rather than replace them: "those that know fewer commands know a subset of the commands used by their more-knowledgeable peers" ([Linton et al. 1998](https://cdn.aaai.org/Workshops/1998/WS-98-08/WS98-08-017.pdf)).

The binding resource is practice, not storage. The probability that an item will be needed falls as a power function of the time since its last use, and memory tracks that statistic ([Anderson & Schooler 1991](https://dyurovsky.github.io/learning-humans-machines/class/13-class/papers/anderson1991.pdf)). In a hotkey study with Zipfian command frequencies, the hotkeys of frequent commands were learnt significantly more often (p < .0001) ([Grossman, Dragicevic & Balakrishnan 2007](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/~ravin/papers/chi2007_learninghotkeys.pdf)). Similar material also interferes. Pooled studies show next-day recall of a list falling from about 80 % with no earlier lists to about 20 % after about 20 earlier lists (secondary: [Memory & Cognition 2010, citing Underwood](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-010-0017-4)). Each further fact attached to one cue slows retrieval of all of them, which is the fan effect ([Fan effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fan_effect)). In a Zipfian command set, most commands are used too rarely to be practised, so they stay unlearnt, and each similar command makes the others harder to recall.

The strongest objection is that experts hold far more. Chess masters are estimated to hold about 50,000 chunks, with a range of 10,000 to 100,000 ([Gobet & Simon 1996](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/chess/1996-gobet.pdf)). One subject raised his digit span from 7 to 79 after more than 230 hours of practice (secondary: [Ericsson, Chase & Faloon, DTIC](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA114634.pdf)). The objection is right about storage. It fails on the conditions, though. Chunks take hundreds to thousands of hours, and they form only where structure recurs: on random chess positions, players of every level recalled only about 3 or 4 pieces (secondary: [notes on Chase & Simon](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/Chase_and_Simon_-_Perception_in_chess)). Every exception or irregular behaviour in a tool is a configuration that will not recur, so it stays a separate element. The expert evidence therefore argues for regularity, not for size.

Recall and recognition differ by a large factor, and users rarely move from one to the other unaided. Word users recognised about twice as many functions as they used ([McGrenere & Moore 2000](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/~joanna/papers/gi_2000_bloat.pdf)). With an ordinary menu, only **28.9 % of trials used the hotkey**. Audio feedback raised this to 66.6 %, and disabling the menu raised it to 72.8 %. A pilot found that "presenting more than 10 items at a time would make it too difficult to learn any hotkeys" in one session of about 40 minutes ([Grossman et al. 2007](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/~ravin/papers/chi2007_learninghotkeys.pdf)). Ten advanced Word users used a shortcut as their main method for **only 11 of 26 common commands**, although 21 had one. For 16 commands, at least half did not know the shortcut, and all of them named "not knowing the shortcut" as the reason ([Hendy, Booth & McGrenere 2010](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~joanna/papers/GI2010_Hendy.pdf)). Marking menus show the alternative. Because the expert gesture rehearses the novice one, marks were 3.5 times faster than menu selection, and one of two logged users made about 93 % of selections as marks. Marks faded after breaks, and fading fell with experience ([Kurtenbach & Buxton 1994](https://research.autodesk.com/app/uploads/2023/03/user-learning-and-performance.pdf_recFBbPwmG7SW9uDe.pdf)). After the first week, the main obstacle is awareness. A protocol in which a coach offered suggestions found **7.55 learnability issues per 15-minute task, against 2.8 for think-aloud**, because users rarely mention what they do not know exists ([Grossman, Fitzmaurice & Attar 2009](https://research.autodesk.com/app/uploads/2023/03/a-survey-of-software.pdf_recqWPTEYd6nsP0r8.pdf)). Self-reports cannot replace logs: AutoCAD professionals used more than three times as many commands as they estimated, and they put the program's size at 367 commands, about a fifth of the real count ([Matejka et al. 2009](https://research.autodesk.com/app/uploads/2023/03/communitycommands-command-recommendations-for.pdf_recZiyrKCEIuGQLZh.pdf)).

A large *available* set does little harm in itself. Only 13 of 53 Word users (25 %) wanted only the functions they used, 24 (45 %) preferred unused ones tucked away, and no group of functions displeased every participant ([McGrenere 2000](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/~joanna/papers/chi2000_poster_paper.pdf)). A simplified "Training Wheels" word processor, which blocked functions that simple tasks did not need, let novices finish significantly faster and with significantly fewer errors (secondary, via [McGrenere & Moore 2000](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/~joanna/papers/gi_2000_bloat.pdf)). The budget therefore limits what a user must hold in order to work. It does not limit what exists, provided the rest can be found when needed.

## A defensible budget: about a dozen concepts, four or five on day one, fifty held items

The measured figures support a tiered budget. Each tier counts a different unit, and each carries its own strength of evidence.

| Tier | What is counted | Budget | Basis | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| One act | interacting elements the user must combine: mode, world, selection, verb, argument, scope | at most 4 | Cowan's 3–5 chunks, central 4; Halford's four-variable ceiling, five at chance | measured in the lab, not in software |
| One session | distinct commands | about 6–9 | GIMP per-session median 6, mean 9 | measured, logs, one program |
| Day one | concepts needed before the first useful task; new commands taught at once | 4–5 concepts; about 10 commands | Grossman's pilot ceiling of 10 new mappings at once; the interaction ceiling, since no concept is yet a chunk; Training Wheels | estimate from a lab pilot and the interaction limit |
| Shared core | commands nearly every user needs | about 12–15 | Word: 12 used regularly by over 75 %; GIMP: 15 used by over half | measured |
| Concepts in total | purpose-level concepts in Jackson's sense | about 10–15 | arithmetic against the held vocabulary (next paragraph) | estimate |
| Held vocabulary | everything recalled: concept names, verbs, object kinds, command shapes, exceptions, personal shortcuts | at most about 50 | per-user vocabularies of 27–50 in four programs | measured, logs and self-report |
| Recognised tail | operations found by search or menu, not recalled | no fixed cap; Word users recognised a mean of 135 | Word familiarity, usage-to-familiarity ratio 57 % | measured, self-report |

The concept figure is the weakest row, and it rests on arithmetic. A research notebook has more distinct purposes than a minimal outliner. Roam builds its core on two concepts, "pages and bullets" (secondary: [Roam MVP review](https://medium.com/bloated-mvp/roam-research-mvp-review-pages-bullets-cool-new-product-category-28baf5dd0a3)), while a notebook that checks claims and compares worlds needs several more. The illustrative factoring in the last section has eight, and that sets the lower end of the range. The ceiling comes from the held vocabulary. Assume, without a source, that each concept carries 3 or 4 named actions of its own. Then 15 concepts already fill 45–60 held items, which is the top of the measured range. More concepts than that push the held set beyond what any measured population sustained, unless the concepts share their verbs through one grammar. Shared verbs give headroom: 15 concepts, 10 shared verbs and 2 command shapes make 27 held items, which leaves room for the user's own frequent extras. Jackson's group reports an industrial inventory of about 150 concepts used by about 250 employees across three major products ([Wilczynski, Gregoire-Wright & Jackson 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.14975)). That is a designers' catalogue, not one learner's load, and it says nothing about a learner's limit.

Against this budget, 231 mechanisms that a learner *must hold* are about 4.6 times the ceiling of 50 held items, and 1.7 times the mean recognition set of Word users. The count is also close to Word 97's whole first-level interface of 265 functions, which no user in that study came near to using. The figure needs a recount before it is judged, however, because it mixes units. Sweller warns that "we cannot determine beforehand, merely by analysing the materials, what constitutes an element", and that "a precise measure of element interactivity that is independent of the learner is unobtainable" ([Sweller 1994](https://mrbartonmaths.com/resourcesnew/8.%20Research/Explicit%20Instruction/CLT,%20learning%20difficulty%20and%20instructional%20design.pdf)). The same 231 could be a fault, or a long tail that no one needs to hold. A recount by fixed rules (next sections) decides which.

The limits of the evidence are concrete. None of the vocabulary studies is of a personal knowledge or research notebook. All come from menu, toolbar or command-line programs of the 1980s to 2000s, and no comparable log study of a keyboard-driven, command-palette program was found. The Word figures are self-report, and AutoCAD users' own estimates undercounted their logged use by more than three times. The studies count commands, so they bound the *surface* directly and the conceptual model only loosely. Individual spread is wide (8 to 119 functions used in Word, 1 to 169 commands in GIMP), so the budget describes a typical user, and a heavy daily researcher could exceed it. The working-memory numbers come from laboratory tasks, not from software. No controlled study shows that designs with fewer or more specific concepts, in Jackson's sense, are learnt faster. No study measures retention of adopted commands over months, beyond Kurtenbach and Buxton's two users. The budget is therefore a hypothesis with measured anchors, and the notebook's own journal can test it.

## Composition multiplies capability only while every part stays regular

The systems that cover many tasks with few concepts share one move. They choose one uniform substrate and make each new capability a composition over it. Ingalls put it as "a minimum set of unchangeable parts; those parts should be as general as possible; and all parts of the system should be held in a uniform framework" ([Ingalls 1981](https://www.cs.virginia.edu/~evans/cs655/readings/smalltalk.html)). Pike and Kernighan rejected a `-v` option on `cat` because it "confuses what cat's job is … with what it happens to do in a common special case". They advised that a frequently needed composition "can be packaged under a name" ([Pike & Kernighan 1984](https://harmful.cat-v.org/cat-v/unix_prog_design.pdf)). In Acme, "any text anywhere in Acme may be a command" ([Pike 1994](https://9p.io/sys/doc/acme/acme.html)). Spreadsheets are the cognitive-dimensions tutorial's example of an "abstraction-hating" system, one with a low abstraction barrier ([Green & Blackwell 1998](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~afb21/CognitiveDimensions/CDtutorial.pdf)). Non-programmers "can be extremely productive with spreadsheets", and they pass advanced features to one another informally ([Nardi & Miller 1990](https://artifex.org/~bonnie/pdf/Nardi_spreadsheet.pdf)). Vim shows the arithmetic. Its 15–16 operators apply to any motion or text object, and counts multiply, so "'2d3w' deletes six words" ([Vim motion.txt](https://vimhelp.org/motion.txt.html)). Operators times its 24 text objects give over 350 commands, which is this report's arithmetic. That is a count of capability, not of learnability, and no study found compares Vim's grammar with a flat list.

The measured support for composition comes from command-language experiments, and it is modest but consistent. Task-Action Grammar predicts that "of two similar task languages, the one that will be the easier to learn and remember is the one with the fewer simple-task rule schemas". In Payne's 1985 experiment, **one consistent structure across all task types was easiest to learn**. A language with one structure per semantic category came second. Worst was a language with two structures that cut across the categories, "even though the TAG notation for Language 3 has the smallest total number of rules" ([Payne & Green 1986](https://typeset.io/pdf/task-action-grammars-a-model-of-the-mental-representation-of-1k271becjq.pdf)). The authors predict that consistency aligned with the user's semantic categories helps, and that "consistencies across groupings of tasks orthogonal to the semantic organization will be of no benefit". Structured namesets, in which every command is a verb such as SEND, FILE, CREATE or MODIFY plus an object name, were easier to learn than unrelated names (Scapin 1982; Carroll 1982). Names in which "opposite commands are invoked by opposite words" were easier too (Carroll 1982). A recurring argument kept in a constant position had weaker support ([Payne & Green 1986](https://typeset.io/pdf/task-action-grammars-a-model-of-the-mental-representation-of-1k271becjq.pdf); [Barnard et al. 1981](https://johnmorton.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2014/11/1981-philb-ii-int-j-m-m-studies.pdf)). Payne and Green report none of these results with effect sizes. The one modern test of a concept redesign is Gitless, which removed Git's staging area and detached head. Eleven participants were faster on branching tasks, significantly so on one (p = 0.003, and p = 0.066 on another). Yet 4 failed a task because they expected `gl branch -c` to switch branches as `git checkout -b` does ([Perez De Rosso & Jackson 2016](https://spderosso.github.io/oopsla16.pdf)). Familiarity helps when a reused name keeps its behaviour, and it misleads when the name keeps only its look.

Regularity is the condition that makes composition cheap. A verb that means the same on every object kind is learnt once and combined without a new interacting element. A verb whose meaning varies by kind adds one element per variant. This follows from Sweller's definition, though no software study has tested it. One name with two meanings is the classic arrangement of one cue with two responses. It produces interference in both directions, and through the fan effect it slows retrieval of both meanings on every use, so its cost exceeds one extra item. Naming makes collisions likely. Two people chose the same term for the same thing with probability **under 0.20**, so access through one designer's word fails 80–90 % of the time, and "unlimited aliasing" gave several-fold gains ([Furnas et al. 1987](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/furnas-landauer-gomez-dumais-thevocabularyprobleminhumansystemcommunication-1987)). Aliasing must run one way. Brennan reports that, in an application of only 25 commands, two people who chose the same term meant the same command by it only 15 % of the time ([Brennan 1996](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~julia/papers/brennan96.pdf)). Jackson's group counts this directly as "conceptual entropy": "the number of concept pairs that involve duplication or confusion", meaning one name with two meanings or one meaning with two names ([Wilczynski et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.14975)). Raskin's monotony, one way to do each task, pulls against Vim's many routes to one result ([Raskin Center](https://www.raskincenter.org/rchi/core-principles)). The two reconcile if monotony holds at the level of concepts and the grammar applies only to edits.

Jackson supplies the unit of count and the tests for factoring. A concept is "free standing" and has a name, a purpose, state, actions and an operational principle ([Jackson, concept cheatsheet](https://essenceofsoftware.com/workshops/autodesk-online/pdfs/concept-cheatsheet.pdf)). A good design maps purposes to concepts one to one. Redundancy means several concepts for one purpose, and overloading means one concept with several ([Jackson, EoS sample chapter](https://essenceofsoftware.com/posts/sample-chapters/eos-intro-sample-chapter.pdf)). Lightroom had "three distinct concepts that all serve the purpose of classifying photos" (Rating, Flag and ColorLabel), and their inconsistent scoping was later "brought into line". A camera's aspect ratio "piggybacked" on Image Size until a split made it its own concept. Synergy is the payoff of a good composition: a Todo concept synchronised with Label marks pending tasks PENDING, so "the user can use the Label concept to perform filterings that might otherwise have needed additional functionality in the Todo concept" ([Jackson, Concept Design Moves](https://people.csail.mit.edu/dnj/publications/nfm-design-moves-22.pdf)). Two named smells apply to a notebook. The first is "Entity", one concept per data type, which yields a "proliferation of many concepts with little value and high coupling". The second is "observer actions", query-only commands that a general query makes redundant ([Jackson, cheatsheet](https://essenceofsoftware.com/workshops/autodesk-online/pdfs/concept-cheatsheet.pdf)). The cognitive-dimensions framework adds the day-one test. The abstraction barrier is "the minimum number of new abstractions that must be mastered before using the system", and the recommended form is "incremental abstractions": a low barrier with built-in shortcuts, open to new abstractions later ([Green & Blackwell 1998](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~afb21/CognitiveDimensions/CDtutorial.pdf)).

## Factor the 231 by purpose, then recount against the budget

Factoring starts with a counting rule, because the budget is meaningless until "one thing to learn" has a definition. The rule below follows the sources. Each line says how much a learner pays for one kind of thing they meet.

| What the learner meets | Counts as | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| A concept: one purpose, with its state and actions | 1 | Jackson's unit of count |
| A verb or an object kind in a regular grammar | 1 each, not 1 per pair | one lexical rule each (Payne & Green) |
| A command shape (rule schema) | 1 each; aim for 1 or 2 | TAG's main complexity index |
| A verb whose meaning changes with the object kind | 1 per variant, plus an interacting element | it cannot be combined without recalling the variant (Sweller) |
| One name with two meanings | more than 2 | interference in both directions, and the fan effect |
| Several names for one meaning (one-way aliases) | about 0, and it helps finding | Furnas; Brennan's warning applies only to the reverse direction |
| A mode the user must track | 1 interacting element in every act under it | Sellen; Raskin |
| An exception or special case | 1, and it never chunks | chunks form only where structure recurs |
| A dialog, a buffer, a record or another interface or storage artefact | 0 concepts; remove it or hide it | Henderson and Johnson exclude "database, dialog box, mode, string" ([Henderson & Johnson](https://courses.dubberly.com/frameworks_2025_fall/Conceptual_Models.pdf)) |

The method then runs in order, because each step changes what the next one counts. Each step has a test, so that "factored" is a checked claim, not an impression.

| Step | Action | Done when | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Sort | Classify each of the 231 as a concept, an operation, an alias, an exception, or an interface or storage artefact. | each entry has exactly one kind | Henderson & Johnson; Sweller |
| 2. Name purposes | Write the user's purposes, and map each mechanism to one of them. | every mechanism has a purpose, or is dropped | Jackson's specificity |
| 3. Fix specificity | Merge concepts that share a purpose. Split concepts that serve several. Add a concept for a purpose that has none. | each purpose maps to one concept, and the conceptual entropy count is 0 | Jackson's design moves; Wilczynski et al. |
| 4. Choose one substrate | Hold every concept's state on one shared structure. Mirror statuses into one label-and-query concept. | one query serves every "list all X" need, and no observer actions remain | Ingalls; Jackson's Todo and Label |
| 5. Write one grammar | Express operations as a verb–object schema that follows the user's semantic categories. Use opposite words for opposite acts and a fixed position for the recurring argument. | 1–2 command shapes; no verb changes meaning across kinds; no grouping cuts across categories | Payne & Green; Scapin; Carroll; Barnard |
| 6. Check each act | Count the interacting elements that each operation requires. Replace modes with quasimodes or visible context. | at most 4 per act, and no state held only in the head | Cowan; Halford; Sellen; Raskin; Acme |
| 7. Tier | Mark a day-one core and a learning path ordered by expected frequency. Leave a recognised tail behind search that matches synonyms. Make the novice route rehearse the expert one. | day one needs at most 5 concepts, and every tail item is found by several words | Linton; Furnas; Kurtenbach & Buxton; Grossman 2007; Green & Blackwell |
| 8. Trace and recount | Map every task behind the 231 to a composed operation, a query or a tail command, then recount held items by the rule above. | 0 unmapped tasks; at most about 50 held items and about 15 concepts | this report's budget |
| 9. Measure | Log use from the journal for each user. | the counts below exist, from logs, not self-report | Grossman et al. 2009; Matejka et al. 2009 |

Step 4 is where most of the saving lies, and Payne's result marks its trap. A substrate lets one query concept answer many tasks, as Label answers Todo's filtering. A grammar that saves rules by grouping tasks *across* the user's categories was harder to learn than one with more rules, so the saving must come from uniformity within the user's categories, not from clever cross-cutting. Step 6 matters most for a notebook that compares worlds. A command inside a supposed world combines the world, a selection, a verb and an argument, which is four elements and already at the ceiling. Holding the world as a quasimode, or showing it at the point of action, takes it out of working memory. That application is an inference from Sellen's and Halford's results, not a tested design.

As an illustration only, and not a tested factoring, the concept-design analysis suggests that a researcher's notebook could rest on about eight behaviour patterns rather than one concept per data type. They are an outline of items that nest, typed links, labels, one query, claims with a status, constraints, worlds, and an append-only journal. Backlinks then come from links at no extra concept cost, which removes the "one-way pointer" that Green and Blackwell name as the textbook hidden dependency ([Green & Blackwell 1998](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~afb21/CognitiveDimensions/CDtutorial.pdf)). Claim status, world membership and constraint violations become labels that the one query filters. Showing a violation beside the edit that caused it turns knock-on viscosity, "one change 'in the head' entails further actions to restore consistency", into a visible fact. Each of the eight must still pass the purpose test of step 2. Adversaria's own design document already contains one instance of step 5. Its outline model has Delete, Insert, Replace, Move and Copy act on a character, word, statement, branch or group, with guide words such as "Copy (from) report (to) archive" that show the argument slots (`docs/design.md`, § The outline model, checked this session). That is 25 operations from 10 names and one shape, so the learner holds 11 items, not 25.

The experiment can then test the budget rather than assume it. From the journal, count for each user the distinct commands per session, the cumulative distinct commands per week, the share of use taken by the top 10, the commands each suggestion introduced, and whether each was used again after 1, 7 and 30 days. The "increase in commands used over certain time interval" is already a standard learnability metric ([Grossman, Fitzmaurice & Attar 2009](https://research.autodesk.com/app/uploads/2023/03/a-survey-of-software.pdf_recqWPTEYd6nsP0r8.pdf)). Prior figures set the expectations. Even the best recommender's suggestions were rated "good" (new to the user, who said they would use it) only 30.9 % of the time ([Matejka et al. 2009](https://research.autodesk.com/app/uploads/2023/03/communitycommands-command-recommendations-for.pdf_recZiyrKCEIuGQLZh.pdf)). One recommender capped its suggestions at about four at a time, which was a design choice, not a measured limit ([Linton et al. 1998](https://cdn.aaai.org/Workshops/1998/WS-98-08/WS98-08-017.pdf)). Blackwell's attention-investment model predicts that users learn a feature when its payoff is visible soon and its risk is low ([Blackwell 2002](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~afb21/publications/HCC02a.pdf)). A permanent journal raises the felt risk of trying things, unless trying is plainly reversible or confined to a supposed world.

## Conclusion

The question "how many features can one person learn" asks for the wrong unit. The binding resource is practice, and practice goes almost entirely to a small, frequent core. Composition matters because it changes which parts get that practice. In a flat set of 231 commands, a command used once a month is always learnt afresh. In a regular grammar, the same rare task is a new combination of a verb and an object kind that the user practises daily, so the rare task borrows practice from the frequent parts. This is why regularity, not the raw count, decides whether a large capability fits a small head. It is also why every exception, homonym and mode costs more than its place on a list suggests: each one is a part that cannot borrow.

For Adversaria, the budget becomes a claim that can fail. If the factoring works, logged personal vocabularies should level off near 30–50 while the tasks covered keep growing, and suggested operations built from known parts should still be in use at 30 days more often than standalone commands. If daily researchers instead sustain well over 50 distinct operations a week with low error, the ceiling is too low for this population. If they never get past the day-one core, the barrier is too high. Each result is a finding. The first test is cheap and comes before any redesign: recount the 231 under the counting rule. That recount holds the project's own figure to the rule it applies to every imported claim: a figure without a stated method is a claim, not a fact.
