## Daniel Jackson's concept design: criteria, counting, overloading, redundancy, synergy, and app counts

### Takeaway
Jackson counts concepts by purpose. A concept is a free-standing unit of behaviour (name, purpose, state, actions, operational principle). A good design maps purposes to concepts one-to-one ("specificity"). It fails in two ways: redundancy, where several concepts serve one purpose, and overloading, where one concept serves several purposes. Concepts compose only through synchronised actions, and a good composition is "synergistic", where one concept amplifies another. Almost all of this is argued from case analyses. The only measured results nearby are a 150-concept industrial inventory and the Gitless user study (11 people, covered under the measured-comparisons question).

### Cited Findings
**Definitions and structure (argued; checked)**
- A concept catalogue entry has a Name ("pithy and mnemonic, one word if possible"), a Purpose, Actions, State ("a data model, what the running concept remembers") and a Principle ("archetypal scenario showing how it's used"). A good definition has a "compelling purpose", "strong actions", "rich state", and is "free standing: easily understood without other concepts" — [Jackson, Concept design cheatsheet (2025)](https://essenceofsoftware.com/workshops/autodesk-online/pdfs/concept-cheatsheet.pdf)
- Two properties set concepts apart from features and classes. First, "each concept is self-contained: its behavior is defined without reference to other concepts, and concepts do not 'use' each other". Second, "concepts are purposive: each brings its own benefit that can be defined and evaluated without reference to another". Jackson calls both "forms of independence". He says this independence "simplif[ies] the user's mental model" and is "essential for its cognitive 'robustness'" — [Jackson, "Concept Design Moves" (NFM 2022)](https://people.csail.mit.edu/dnj/publications/nfm-design-moves-22.pdf)
- Self-containment means end-to-end function. The Label concept covers "not only adding and removing labels, but actually using the labels to find items" — [Jackson, Concept Design Moves](https://people.csail.mit.edu/dnj/publications/nfm-design-moves-22.pdf)
- The cheatsheet names the composition mechanism "Action sync". Its list of new ideas includes "Concepts as behavior patterns not data entities", "Decoupling for full independent development" and "Emphasis on reuse of familiar concepts" — [Cheatsheet](https://essenceofsoftware.com/workshops/autodesk-online/pdfs/concept-cheatsheet.pdf)
- The 2015 essay pairs each concept with "one compelling purpose and an archetypal scenario, the operational principle". It finds flaws through "a small set of conditions, mainly about how concepts relate to their purposes". It arranges concepts in a dependence graph and takes coherent subsets of that graph to describe a product family — [Jackson, "Towards a theory of conceptual design for software", Onward! 2015, DSpace record](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/106499) (abstract, via snippet)

**The book's criteria (argued; checked from the table of contents and index of the 2018 draft)**
- *The Essence of Software* has three principle chapters: "9 Concept Specificity", "10 Concept Familiarity" and "11 Concept Integrity". Its essentials chapters cover structure, purposes, composition, dependence and mapping — [EoS sample chapter, 2018 draft](https://essenceofsoftware.com/posts/sample-chapters/eos-intro-sample-chapter.pdf)
- The index files specificity under "one-to-one with purpose 127–145". It gives four kinds of overloading: "false convergence", "denied purpose", "emergent purpose" and "piggybacking". It also has entries for "redundant 130–132", "purposeless 127, 144" and "purpose … without concept 127–129". Integrity (157–164) is tied to "operational principle … violated 158" and to synchronisation. "Freestanding" (99–107) means a concept can be understood without the others — [EoS sample chapter](https://essenceofsoftware.com/posts/sample-chapters/eos-intro-sample-chapter.pdf)
- Synergy is indexed as "synergistic composition 88–90" with examples "Apple Trash 90", "Gmail 89, 114", "Photoshop 90", "Outlook", "Netflix", "Safari", "imperfect [synergy] 90, 91–92". The index also has "overloading … synergy and, 271" — a link between the two — and "synchronization … over 92–94 … under 94–96" (over- and under-synchronisation) — [EoS sample chapter](https://essenceofsoftware.com/posts/sample-chapters/eos-intro-sample-chapter.pdf)
- Applications indexed (by page, not by concept count): Dropbox 17–23, 40, 50, 203–206; Git 134, 204, 254; Gitless 254; Gmail 37, 50, 89, 93, 114–116, 123, 128, 130, 132, 136–139; Facebook; Backblaze; Emacs; BBEdit — [EoS sample chapter](https://essenceofsoftware.com/posts/sample-chapters/eos-intro-sample-chapter.pdf)
- From the slides: specificity means each purpose maps to exactly one concept. Redundancy is more than one concept per purpose, and overloading is more than one purpose per concept. The Gmail Label example tests redundancy by asking whether one concept would suffice — [Jackson, "5 design moves" slides](https://essenceofsoftware.com/workshops/autodesk-boston/pdfs/5-design-moves.pdf) (snippet)

**Design moves: how to factor (argued; checked)**
- There are "six design moves, organized into three pairs of duals", each a trade-off. **Split/merge** trades "simplicity and directness of usage" against "flexibility". **Unify/specialize** trades "generality and specificity"; Jackson's example is the adjustable wrench, where "one single concept replaced multiple variants". **Tighten/loosen** trades "automation and control"; the example is an aircraft toilet, where the light and the lock are synchronised — [Concept Design Moves](https://people.csail.mit.edu/dnj/publications/nfm-design-moves-22.pdf)
- Synergy through deliberate redundancy: a Todo concept is synchronised with Label so that pending tasks are labelled PENDING. "You might think at first that this redundancy brings little benefit. But actually there's a valuable synergy at play … the user can use the Label concept to perform filterings that might otherwise have needed additional functionality in the Todo concept" — [Concept Design Moves](https://people.csail.mit.edu/dnj/publications/nfm-design-moves-22.pdf)
- Piggybacking: in a camera, the aspect ratio was folded into the Image Size concept. Jackson calls this "'overloading by piggybacking' … the developer needed to find a place to insert the ratio feature and 'piggybacked' it onto another concept". His fix is a split that makes Ratio its own concept — [Concept Design Moves](https://people.csail.mit.edu/dnj/publications/nfm-design-moves-22.pdf)
- Redundant specialisation: Adobe Lightroom has "three distinct concepts that all serve the purpose of classifying photos into a small number of fixed categories" (Rating, Flag, ColorLabel). They are used "mostly in one particular scenario". In an early version, flags were scoped by collection while ratings and labels were scoped by file. That inconsistency was later "brought into line", "presumably because users found it confusing" — [Concept Design Moves](https://people.csail.mit.edu/dnj/publications/nfm-design-moves-22.pdf)
- "Software designers tend to favor unification over specialization, often to reduce implementation effort" — [Concept Design Moves](https://people.csail.mit.edu/dnj/publications/nfm-design-moves-22.pdf)

**Named flaws (argued; checked)**
- Ten flaws: #1 Conflation ("should not conflate separable functions"); #2 Fragmentation ("includes only part of a function, doesn't deliver full value"); #3 Coupling ("references to external objects [should be] fully generic"); #4 Not user facing; #5 User-interface coupling; #6 Missing actions; #7 Inadequate state; #8 Not invented here (example: PowerPoint's Section concept, "a complex mechanism for a familiar purpose"; fix: "Use a familiar and well-known concept instead"); #9 Crossing boundary; #10 Observer action (query-only actions; drop them, because "synchronizations can query the concept state") — [Cheatsheet](https://essenceofsoftware.com/workshops/autodesk-online/pdfs/concept-cheatsheet.pdf)
- Five bad smells: Single object; Vague purpose; UI component (a "Settings concept" that bundles Password, Profile, Preference and Session); Entity (one concept per ontology entity, for example "Account, Transaction, Withdrawal, Deposit", which leads to a "proliferation of many concepts with little value and high coupling"); Phase — [Cheatsheet](https://essenceofsoftware.com/workshops/autodesk-online/pdfs/concept-cheatsheet.pdf)

**Counting and entropy (an industrial count; checked)**
- Wilczynski, Gregoire-Wright and Jackson define "conceptual entropy" in its simplest form as "the number of concept pairs that involve duplication or confusion: two concepts that share the same name but have different meanings, or that each share the same meaning but have different names". They add that "loss of uniformity and clarity also contribute". They cite Brooks: "Conceptual Integrity is the most important consideration in system design" — [Concept-Centric Software Development (arXiv 2304.14975)](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.14975)
- In one company's concept inventory, "the concept repository currently holds around 150 concepts, and is used by around 250 employees to refine, and align, the design of three major products" — [arXiv 2304.14975](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.14975)

**Git as a worked example (case analysis plus a small study; checked)**
- Gitless "has no staging area, and the only file classifications are 'tracked,' 'untracked,' 'ignored,' and 'in conflict'". It "eliminates the concept of a file having a staged version". There is "no possible way of getting in a detached head state". Stashing "does have a single, coherent motivating purpose", but the authors find its link to the purposes of version control weak. They conclude it may "patch flaws in the design of Git" rather than meet a requirement — [Perez De Rosso & Jackson, OOPSLA 2016](https://spderosso.github.io/oopsla16.pdf)

### Inferences
- Jackson's unit of count is the purpose, not the command, the screen or the entity. To count a design's concepts, list its purposes. Then check each purpose against concepts: zero concepts means a missing concept, more than one is redundancy, and one concept with several purposes is overloading. This gives a falsifiable count, which suits a notebook that treats its own claims as claims.
- The "Entity" bad smell warns against a notebook factoring that has one concept per data type (item, link, claim, belief, constraint, world, journal entry). The behaviour patterns may be fewer: for example Outline, Reference/Link, Label, Assertion-with-status, Constraint check, Branch/World, Append-only log. This is my inference, not Jackson's.
- The Todo+Label example shows when redundancy pays: one concept's state is mirrored into a general concept (Label) that already has strong query actions. A notebook could mirror claim status, world membership and constraint violations as labels or facts that one query concept can filter. One query concept would then serve many tasks.
- Flaw #10 (observer actions) argues against per-concept "list all X" commands when one general query mechanism exists.

### Gaps
- I found no published concept counts for Dropbox, Gmail or Git as whole apps. The book indexes them by page, and I could not read those pages (the book is in copyright; I did not use unofficial copies). Recalled: the book analyses Dropbox's Folder/Sharing confusion and Gmail's Label/Category overlap. Not checked.

Both are consistent with the index entries, but neither is checked.
- I found no controlled study showing that designs with fewer or more specific concepts in Jackson's sense are learnt faster. Gitless is the closest, and it is small.

## Conceptual models: Johnson & Henderson, Norman's system image, Green & Petre's cognitive dimensions

### Takeaway
Johnson and Henderson give a procedure. List the user-exposed objects, their attributes and operations, and the relations among them (subtype, part-of, containment). Exclude implementation objects. Aim for "Less is more!". Green's cognitive dimensions give a vocabulary for the costs of a notation. One dimension, the abstraction barrier, is literally a concept count: "the minimum number of new abstractions that must be mastered before using the system". The framework is broad and its authors say it is hard to turn into metrics.

### Cited Findings
- Henderson and Johnson's goals for a conceptual model: "Simple: 'Less is more!'" and "Focus on task domain". The method is an "Object/Operation Analysis": "Objects: User-exposed concepts; Attributes of each object-type; Operations on each object-type; Relationships between objects: What is a subtype of what? What is a part of what? What can contain what?" — [Henderson & Johnson, "Conceptual Models: Core to the Design of Interactive Applications" (slides, 2025)](https://courses.dubberly.com/frameworks_2025_fall/Conceptual_Models.pdf) (argued; checked)
- Their inclusion test, from a bank example. Objects: "Yes: customer, account, debit card. No: database, dialog box, mode, string". Operations: "Yes: register, unregister, deposit funds. No: click button, load database, edit table row, create record, flush buffer". So concepts are task-domain things, not UI or storage things — [Henderson & Johnson slides](https://courses.dubberly.com/frameworks_2025_fall/Conceptual_Models.pdf) (checked)
- Publication history from the same slides: an *Interactions* article (Jan–Feb 2002); a CHI tutorial "Designing What to Design: a Task-Focused Conceptual Model" (2009–2019); *Conceptual Models: Core to Good Design* (Morgan & Claypool, 2011); and a later Springer book — [Henderson & Johnson slides](https://courses.dubberly.com/frameworks_2025_fall/Conceptual_Models.pdf) (checked)
- Viscosity is "resistance to change: the cost of making small changes". **Repetition viscosity**: "a single goal-related operation … requires an undue number of individual actions". **Knock-on viscosity**: "one change 'in the head' entails further actions to restore consistency". Example: inserting a figure renumbers all later figures and their cross-references — [Green & Blackwell, Cognitive Dimensions tutorial v1.2 (1998)](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~afb21/CognitiveDimensions/CDtutorial.pdf) (argued; checked)
- Hidden dependency: "a relationship between two components such that one of them is dependent on the other, but that the dependency is not fully visible. In particular, the one-way pointer, where A points to B but B does not contain a back-pointer to A" — [CD tutorial](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~afb21/CognitiveDimensions/CDtutorial.pdf) (checked)
- Premature commitment / enforced lookahead: "Constraints on the order of doing things force the user to make a decision before the proper information is available" — [CD tutorial](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~afb21/CognitiveDimensions/CDtutorial.pdf) (checked)
- Consistency: "similar semantics are expressed in similar syntactic forms" — [CD tutorial](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~afb21/CognitiveDimensions/CDtutorial.pdf) (checked)
- Abstraction barrier: "An abstraction is a class of entities, or a grouping of elements to be treated as one entity, either to lower the viscosity or to make the notation more like the user's conceptual structure. The abstraction barrier is determined by the minimum number of new abstractions that must be mastered before using the system". Systems are classed as abstraction-hating ("Spreadsheets"), abstraction-tolerant, or abstraction-hungry (Smalltalk: "to start writing a program, you first modify the class hierarchy") — [CD tutorial](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~afb21/CognitiveDimensions/CDtutorial.pdf) (checked)
- Recommended workaround: "Incremental abstractions … a system that has a low abstraction barrier but yet contains useful built-in abstractions as short-cuts, and tolerates the addition of useful new abstractions". Trade-off: adding abstractions to reduce viscosity "increases need for lookahead … raises the abstraction barrier; may also increase hidden dependencies" — [CD tutorial](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~afb21/CognitiveDimensions/CDtutorial.pdf) (checked)
- Other dimensions defined: closeness of mapping ("closeness of representation to domain"; LabVIEW is a close mapping, "minimising the number of new concepts that need be learnt"); role-expressiveness ("the purpose of a component … is readily inferred"); visibility and juxtaposability ("ability to place any two components side by side") — [CD tutorial](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~afb21/CognitiveDimensions/CDtutorial.pdf) (checked)
- Self-assessment: the framework "succeeds in its aim of being broad-brush, it pays by being too unspecific, making it hard to metricate the dimensions or even to define exactly what they mean" — [CD tutorial](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~afb21/CognitiveDimensions/CDtutorial.pdf) (checked)
- Jackson's book indexes "system image 26" and "gulfs of execution and evaluation 252–254", so it ties concept design to Norman's model — [EoS sample chapter (index)](https://essenceofsoftware.com/posts/sample-chapters/eos-intro-sample-chapter.pdf) (checked)

### Inferences
- The abstraction barrier can serve as the notebook's day-one concept budget: the concepts a researcher must learn before the first useful task. "Incremental abstractions" argues for a small mandatory core with optional concepts learnt later (see Nardi under the next question).
- A one-way link (A cites B, B shows nothing) is the textbook hidden dependency. Backlinks or "linked references" in Roam-style tools remove it at no extra concept cost if the link concept already exists.
- Knock-on viscosity is what constraint checking addresses: an edit violates a constraint elsewhere, and the user must find it. Showing violations next to the edit turns a hidden knock-on into a visible one.
- The Henderson–Johnson inclusion test (no "dialog box, mode … create record") is a direct filter for a mechanism inventory. A mechanism that names a UI or storage artefact is not a concept.

### Gaps
- I did not read Green & Petre (1996, J. Visual Languages and Computing) itself. I used Green & Blackwell's 1998 tutorial, by the same group, which defines the same dimensions. The 1996 paper's "abstraction gradient" wording is not checked.
- I did not read Norman's own definition of the system image (*The Design of Everyday Things*). Recalled: the system image is everything the user can perceive of the system (interface, documentation, behaviour), and it is the designer's only channel for conveying the design model to the user's mental model. Not checked.
- I found no measured cognitive-dimensions study that compares concept counts.

## Orthogonality and composition: Unix, Plan 9/Acme, Oberon, Smalltalk, spreadsheets, Vim, Raskin, Engelbart

### Takeaway
The systems that cover many tasks with few concepts share one move. They choose one uniform substrate: text or files (Unix, Acme, Oberon), objects and messages (Smalltalk), cells and formulas (spreadsheets), or operators applied to motions (Vim). Each new capability is then a composition of existing parts, not a new concept. Ingalls states it as "a minimum set of unchangeable parts … as general as possible … held in a uniform framework". Vim's grammar shows the arithmetic: 16 operators and some dozens of motions and text objects give products of the two, and counts multiply as well. All of this is argued or descriptive; none of these sources measures learnability.

### Cited Findings
**Smalltalk (Ingalls, Byte 1981; argued; checked)**
- "Good Design: A system should be built with a minimum set of unchangeable parts; those parts should be as general as possible; and all parts of the system should be held in a uniform framework" — [Ingalls, "Design Principles Behind Smalltalk"](https://www.cs.virginia.edu/~evans/cs655/readings/smalltalk.html)
- "Uniform Metaphor: A language should be designed around a powerful metaphor that can be uniformly applied in all areas. Examples … LISP, which is built on the model of linked structures; APL … arrays; and Smalltalk … communicating objects" — [Ingalls](https://www.cs.virginia.edu/~evans/cs655/readings/smalltalk.html)
- "Factoring: Each independent component in a system would appear in only one place." "Leverage: When a system is well factored, great leverage is available to users and implementers alike." "Modularity: No component in a complex system should depend on the internal details of any other component." "Polymorphism: A program should specify only the behavior of objects, not their representation" — [Ingalls](https://www.cs.virginia.edu/~evans/cs655/readings/smalltalk.html)
- "Reactive Principle: Every component accessible to the user should be able to present itself in a meaningful way for observation and manipulation." "Operating System: An operating system is a collection of things that don't fit into a language. There shouldn't be one" — [Ingalls](https://www.cs.virginia.edu/~evans/cs655/readings/smalltalk.html)

**Unix (Pike & Kernighan 1984; argued; checked)**
- "Much of the power of the UNIX operating system comes from a style of program design that makes programs easy to use and, more important, easy to combine with other programs" — [Pike & Kernighan, "Program design in the UNIX environment"](https://harmful.cat-v.org/cat-v/unix_prog_design.pdf)
- On adding `-v` to `cat`: "Such a modification confuses what cat's job is — concatenating files — with what it happens to do in a common special case … A UNIX program should do one thing well, and leave unrelated tasks to other programs." Line numbering is "readily done with existing tools like sed and awk", and "If line-numbering is needed often, this command can be packaged under a name" — [Pike & Kernighan](https://harmful.cat-v.org/cat-v/unix_prog_design.pdf)
- The main advantage of the Unix approach: "the surrounding environment — the shell and the programs it can invoke — provides a uniform access to system facilities". Options to `cat -v` "require an entire sub-language within the -v option" — [Pike & Kernighan](https://harmful.cat-v.org/cat-v/unix_prog_design.pdf)

**Acme and Oberon (Pike 1994; argued; checked)**
- "Acme instead provides its clients with a fixed user interface and simple conventions to encourage its uniform use. Clients access the facilities of Acme through a file system interface" — [Pike, "Acme: A User Interface for Programmers" (Winter USENIX 1994)](https://9p.io/sys/doc/acme/acme.html)
- "The middle button executes commands … A modest number of commands are recognized as built-ins: words like Cut, Paste, and New … the tags are not menus: any text anywhere in Acme may be a command" — [Pike 1994](https://9p.io/sys/doc/acme/acme.html)
- The right button: "The indicated text is not a command, however, but the argument of a generalized search operator". If it names a file, Acme opens it; if not, Acme searches for it as literal text. Pike: "The effect is a natural overloading of the button to refer to plain text as well as file names" — [Pike 1994](https://9p.io/sys/doc/acme/acme.html)
- Context replaces a mode: "Acme has no single notion of 'current directory'. Instead, every command, file name, action, and so on is interpreted or executed in the directory named by the tag of the window containing the command" — [Pike 1994](https://9p.io/sys/doc/acme/acme.html)
- Chords: "Acme binds some actions to chords of mouse buttons. These include Cut and Paste so these common operations can be done without moving the mouse" — [Pike 1994](https://9p.io/sys/doc/acme/acme.html)
- On lineage: Cedar was "the major inspiration for Oberon … a system of similar scope but much smaller scale". "Acme … applies some of the ideas distilled by Oberon. Where Oberon uses objects and modules within a programming language … Acme uses files and commands within an existing operating system". Global editing is left to external pipelines in `/acme/edit` (e.g. `e file | x '/regexp/' | c 'replacement'`) — [Pike 1994](https://9p.io/sys/doc/acme/acme.html)

**Vim's operator + motion grammar (descriptive; counted from the manual)**
- "The motion commands can be used after an operator command, to have the command operate on the text that was moved over." Vim 9.2 lists 16 operators: `c d y ~ g~ gu gU ! = gq gw g? > < zf g@` (`~` acts as an operator only with `'tildeop'`, so 15 by default). "If the motion includes a count and the operator also had a count before it, the two counts are multiplied … '2d3w' deletes six words." A doubled operator acts on lines ("3dd deletes three lines") — [Vim reference manual, motion.txt (Vim 9.2)](https://vimhelp.org/motion.txt.html) (counted)
- Text objects in the same file include `aw iw aW iW as is ap ip a] a) a> a} ab aB at it a'` and the backtick-quote object, each with an "a" (around) and "i" (inner) form — [motion.txt](https://vimhelp.org/motion.txt.html) (checked by text search for 11 of 12 kinds; the double-quote object is recalled)

**Spreadsheets (Nardi & Miller 1990; ethnography, not an experiment; checked)**
- "Non-programmers may never learn to program recursive functions and nested loops, but they can be extremely productive with spreadsheets." Advanced features (macros, date–time functions, nested conditionals) spread by "Experienced users teach[ing] less experienced users … informally, not in training classes" — [Nardi & Miller, "An Ethnographic Study of Distributed Problem Solving in Spreadsheet Development", CSCW 90](https://artifex.org/~bonnie/pdf/Nardi_spreadsheet.pdf)
- Spreadsheets are the CD tutorial's example of an "abstraction-hating" system, one with a low abstraction barrier — [CD tutorial](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~afb21/CognitiveDimensions/CDtutorial.pdf)

**Raskin (argued)**
- A mode is "a state in which the same user action produces a different result than it would in another state". The rule: "eliminate all modes. If a mode cannot be eliminated, convert it to a quasimode", that is, "a state that can only be maintained through continuous user action" (the Shift key). Archy "used LEAP for navigation — a search-based, keyboard-driven system that was consistent everywhere" — [Raskin Center, Core principles of humane interface design](https://www.raskincenter.org/rchi/core-principles) (checked; secondary summary by Raskin's centre)
- Monotony: a sub-task should have one way to perform it, so that the user never chooses among gestures. Raskin argues this speeds the formation of habits. He rejects separate beginner and expert modes — [Raskin, *The Humane Interface*, ch. 3, "Meanings, Modes, Monotony, and Myths"](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/humane-interface-the/0201379376/0201379376_ch03.html) (snippet; exact wording not checked)

**Engelbart NLS (descriptive; weak sources)**
- Engelbart's 1973 paper describes a command language of alphabetic sequences entered by keyboard or chord keyset. He argues that for frequent functions the "payoff in higher efficiency warrants the extra training costs", which allows "highly abbreviated, non-mnemonic command terms" — [Engelbart, "Design Considerations for Knowledge Workshop Terminals" (1973)](https://www.dougengelbart.org/pubs/papers/scanned-original/1973-augment-14851-Design-Considerations-for-Knowledge-Workshop-Terminals.pdf) (snippet)
- A practitioner's recollection describes verb + noun commands: verbs such as copy, insert, delete and jump; nouns such as word, statement, branch and link, each noun with its own selection rule — [Maynard, "IDE Nostalgia"](https://davidsmaynard.com/keyset/) (secondary; snippet)

### Inferences
- The pattern across Smalltalk, Unix, Acme and spreadsheets: pick one substrate that every concept reads and writes, then make each capability a composition over it. For the notebook, the outline of items, read as facts in Prolog, is the obvious candidate substrate. Links, tags, claim status and world membership as facts (some already done, per the project's task list) follow this pattern.
- Vim's grammar turns a learning cost of n + m into a capability of about n × m. With Vim's 15–16 operators and 24 text objects alone, that is over 350 operator–object commands, before any motions are counted. This is my arithmetic. The capability count is not a learnability measure; the measured evidence is under the next question.
- Acme's "any text anywhere may be a command" and its overloaded right button show how one gesture can serve several purposes without adding a concept, as long as the interpretation is predictable from context. That is a deliberate overloading of a gesture, not of a concept in Jackson's sense.
- Raskin's monotony and Vim's grammar pull in opposite directions. Vim gives many ways to reach one result (`dw`, `de`, `daw`); Raskin wants one. A notebook can keep monotony at the level of concepts (one way to link, one way to assert) while allowing a composable grammar for edits.

### Gaps
- McIlroy's 1978 summary ("Write programs that do one thing and do it well. Write programs to work together. Write programs to handle text streams, because that is a universal interface") is recalled. I did not check it against the *Bell System Technical Journal* or McIlroy's later quotation of it.
- I did not read Wirth & Gutknecht's *Project Oberon* directly. Recalled: in Oberon any displayed text of the form `Module.Command` can be executed with a mouse click, and text viewers are the main interface. Pike's paper confirms only the lineage.
- HyperCard (stacks, cards, backgrounds, fields, buttons, HyperTalk) and Emacs (buffers, windows, modes, keymaps, Lisp functions) are recalled; I found no primary source this session.
- Nardi's book *A Small Matter of Programming* (1993) was not available. A secondary source in search results said Nardi found that spreadsheet users use fewer than ten functions. I could not trace it to a page, so it is unverified.
- I found no formal study of NLS command learnability.

## Measured comparisons: is a composable grammar easier to learn per capability than a flat list?

### Takeaway
Yes, with modest but real support, mostly from 1980s command-language experiments. Payne and Green's Task-Action Grammar predicts that the language with fewer "simple-task rule schemas" is easier to learn. Payne (1985) found that one consistent structure across all task categories beat one structure per category. Both beat two structures that cut across the semantic categories, even though that last language had the fewest total rules. Structured namesets (Scapin 1982; Carroll 1982) and congruent names (Carroll 1982) were easier to learn. The one modern test of concept redesign, Gitless (n = 11), found faster branching tasks: one task reached significance (p = 0.003), and participants reported less frustration. I found no study that measures Vim's operator + motion grammar against a flat command list.

### Cited Findings
- TAG's core claim: "Simple complexity metrics over task-action grammars make predictions about the relative learnability of different task language designs." Its support comes "from the existing empirical literature on command language learning, and from two unreported experiments" — [Payne & Green, "Task-Action Grammars", HCI 2(2), 1986, pp. 93–133](https://typeset.io/pdf/task-action-grammars-a-model-of-the-mental-representation-of-1k271becjq.pdf) (checked)
- The metric: "The most important index of complexity derived from a TAG definition is the number of simple-task rule schemas … For two languages that possess the same number of simple-task rule schemas, complexity comparisons should utilize the total number of rule-schemas". The prediction: "of two similar task languages, the one that will be the easier to learn and remember is the one with the fewer simple-task rule schemas" — [Payne & Green 1986](https://typeset.io/pdf/task-action-grammars-a-model-of-the-mental-representation-of-1k271becjq.pdf) (argued metric; checked)
- Why consistency matters: "Consistency is difficult to define and therefore difficult to measure, but it is informally recognized to be a major determinant of learnability. The advantages of consistency lie in facilitating generalizations by the user, who having learned some parts of the system can then infer others" — [Payne & Green 1986](https://typeset.io/pdf/task-action-grammars-a-model-of-the-mental-representation-of-1k271becjq.pdf) (checked)
- **Measured (Payne 1985, as reported by Payne & Green 1986):** subjects learnt a toy "lost property office" system with three task categories (database queries, messages, games). "The best syntax utilized a consistent higher-level structure … for all possible tasks (Language 1)". "A design which incorporated only two different syntactic command structures, but which used these for groups of task-types that cut across the major categories (Language 3), was harder to learn than a design using three separate command structures, one for each of the categories (Language 2)". "Counting the number of simple-task rule schemas predicts the observed results … even though the TAG notation for Language 3 has the smallest total number of rules" — [Payne & Green 1986](https://typeset.io/pdf/task-action-grammars-a-model-of-the-mental-representation-of-1k271becjq.pdf) (checked; effect sizes not given in this paper)
- Prediction about alignment: "syntactic consistencies across entire semantic categories will be appreciated by users, as will syntactic consistencies within semantic categories, but consistencies across groupings of tasks orthogonal to the semantic organization will be of no benefit" — [Payne & Green 1986](https://typeset.io/pdf/task-action-grammars-a-model-of-the-mental-representation-of-1k271becjq.pdf) (checked)
- **Measured (Scapin 1982, Carroll 1982):** "The advantage of using structured namesets, as demonstrated by Scapin (1982) and Carroll (1982), also can be captured". In Scapin's structured nameset, every command is a verb from {SEND, FILE, CREATE, MODIFY} plus an object name, written as one schema `Task[Operation, Object] → name1[Operation] + name2[Object]` with one rule per verb. The unstructured nameset (TRANSMIT, CLASSIFY, STORE, WRITE, FORM, MAKE, TAKE, CORRECT, TRANSFORM, CHANGE …) needs one rule per operation–object pair — [Payne & Green 1986, Fig. 7](https://typeset.io/pdf/task-action-grammars-a-model-of-the-mental-representation-of-1k271becjq.pdf) (checked)
- **Measured (Carroll 1982):** "subjects learning a pencil-and-paper simulation game found the names of Language A easier to learn than those of Language B … opposite commands are invoked by opposite words" (congruence) — [Payne & Green 1986](https://typeset.io/pdf/task-action-grammars-a-model-of-the-mental-representation-of-1k271becjq.pdf) (checked, as reported)
- **Measured but mixed (Barnard et al. 1981):** the study varied argument order in command strings. It looked at three things: a recurring argument kept in a consistent position, entry order matching natural-language order, and entry order matching screen position. Study I had software specialists design command structures; Study II had naive subjects choose between versions. Effects of a consistent recurring argument depended on its coming first — [Barnard, Hammond, Morton & Long, "Consistency and compatibility in human-computer dialogue", IJMMS 1981](https://johnmorton.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2014/11/1981-philb-ii-int-j-m-m-studies.pdf) (snippet). Payne & Green call it "hard to interpret, but it offers some support for the principle of placing a common parameter in a constant serial position" — [Payne & Green 1986](https://typeset.io/pdf/task-action-grammars-a-model-of-the-mental-representation-of-1k271becjq.pdf) (checked)
- The follow-up experiment is Payne & Green, "The Structure of Command Languages: An Experiment on Task-Action Grammar", IJMMS 30 (1989) 213–234 — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1016%2FS0020-7373%2889%2980011-2) (bibliographic only; results not read)
- **Measured (Gitless):** 11 participants (3 undergraduates, 2 graduates, 3 researchers, 3 engineers); within-subjects; 6 tasks per session. "Most participants completed tasks 3, 4, and 5 (which are all branching related) faster when using Gitless than when using Git. A paired t-test found the differences in task 5 to be significant (t=3.95, df=10, p=0.003). For task 4 the result was p=0.066." "Overall, participants did better using Gitless". In Task 2, 4 participants failed with Gitless because they assumed `gl branch -c` also switched branch, as `git checkout -b` does. The authors' threats to validity: "only 11 people" — [Perez De Rosso & Jackson, OOPSLA 2016](https://spderosso.github.io/oopsla16.pdf) (checked)

### Inferences
- The best-supported rule for a notebook command set: use one verb–object schema across all object kinds, aligned with the user's semantic categories (items, links, claims, worlds). Payne's Language 3 result warns that a grammar which saves rules by cutting across semantic categories can be harder to learn than one with more rules.
- TAG gives a countable measure for the coming factoring task: count simple-task rule schemas (how many different command shapes a user must know), not commands. A flat list of n × m commands needs about n × m schemas. A verb–object grammar needs one schema plus n + m lexical rules.
- The Gitless failure on `gl branch -c` is a familiarity effect: users carried over a Git expectation. That backs Jackson's familiarity principle and warns against reusing a familiar name with different behaviour.

### Gaps
- I found no controlled study of Vim's operator + motion grammar against a flat command set, and no study of learning per capability for any composable editor. That is a real gap, not a search failure on my part: several queries found nothing.
- I could not read the results of Payne & Green 1989, Payne 1985, Scapin 1982, Carroll 1982 or Grudin & Barnard 1984 ("The cognitive demands of learning and representing command names"). I have only Payne & Green's 1986 report of them, with no effect sizes.
- The Gitless study tests a concept redesign, not a grammar. Its sample (11) and single significant task limit it.

## Personal knowledge tools: concept counts and minimal designs

### Takeaway
The minimal outliners build almost everything from one or two concepts: a block (bullet) that nests, and a page that a block can reference. Backlinks are derived from references at no extra concept cost. I found no published concept counts for Org-mode, Workflowy, Roam, Obsidian, Logseq, Tinderbox or Zettelkasten; the sources found are reviews and user accounts.

### Cited Findings
- Roam was described as built on "pages and bullets", its "atomic building blocks", and the reviewer argues that "the data model is their key differentiator, not UI design" — [Roam Research MVP review (Medium)](https://medium.com/bloated-mvp/roam-research-mvp-review-pages-bullets-cool-new-product-category-28baf5dd0a3) (secondary; snippet)
- "Every individual bullet point in Roam is its own 'block' and can be referenced much like a page" — [The Sweet Setup, Roam for Bible study](https://thesweetsetup.com/how-im-using-roam-research-for-bible-study/) (user account; snippet)
- At first glance Roam "appears to be a Workflowy-type online outliner, but first impressions can be deceptive" — [SitePoint, Roam beginner's guide](https://www.sitepoint.com/roam-research-beginners-guide/) (secondary; snippet)

### Inferences
- The Roam pattern fits Jackson's synergy: Outline (blocks nest) plus Reference (blocks and pages link) gives backlinks, linked references, tag pages and query-by-reference without new concepts. A notebook that already has items, `[[links]]` and `#tags` as facts has the same synergy available.
- For a researcher's notebook, the concepts beyond the minimal outliner (claims and beliefs, constraints, worlds, journal) should each meet Jackson's test: a distinct purpose that no existing concept serves. Otherwise they should be labels or facts over the existing outline.

### Gaps
- No primary or measured concept counts found for any PKM tool. Recalled, unchecked descriptions:
  - Workflowy's design centres on one nested bullet with zoom, tags and mirrors.
  - Org-mode layers headings, TODO states, tags, properties, timestamps, agenda views, links, tables and Babel source blocks on one outline.
  - Logseq follows Roam (blocks, pages, references, properties, queries).
  - Obsidian is file-and-page-centred (Markdown notes, wikilinks, backlinks, tags, folders, plugins).
  - Tinderbox has notes with attributes, prototypes, agents and maps.
  - Zettelkasten (Luhmann) uses atomic notes with fixed IDs, links and a register of keywords.
  I could not check any of these with an official source in this session's tool budget. A follow-up should read each tool's own manual and count concepts by Jackson's purpose test.
