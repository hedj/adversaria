# Cognitive capacity: how many concepts, commands and mechanisms one person can hold, learn and use fluently in a tool

Evidence tags on each finding. **[checked]**: I read the claim in the full text of the primary source. **[abstract]**: from the primary source's abstract or bibliographic record only. **[secondary]**: from a secondary account (encyclopaedia, citing paper, course page, mailing list). Claims I could only recall are in the Gaps lists, not in the findings. The application in view: one researcher, a personal knowledge notebook, daily use, keyboard and mouse.

## 1. Measured limits of working memory, and what a user can hold during one task

### Takeaway
With rehearsal and recoding blocked, adults hold about 3 to 5 chunks (central estimate 4); Miller's "seven" is a span measured with rehearsal and recoding allowed, and Miller himself called the recurrence of seven a possible coincidence. When items must be *related* to each other in one judgement, the measured ceiling is about 4 interacting variables, and 5 falls to chance.

### Cited Findings
- **Miller (1956), absolute judgement:** across one-dimensional stimuli, channel capacity averaged 2.6 bits (SD 0.6 bit), "about 6.5 categories". This is a limit on discriminating values along one dimension, not on memory. [checked] — [Miller 1956, Psychological Review](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Miller/)
- **Miller (1956), immediate memory span:** "about seven items" for many materials; Hayes's data gave about 9 binary digits but about 5 monosyllabic words, so span is limited by the number of items (chunks), not by bits. [checked] — [Miller 1956](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Miller/)
- **Miller (1956), recoding:** Smith, with practised 4:1 and 5:1 recodings, recalled about 40 binary digits; recoding "helped in every case, though less than expected", because learning the code took time. [checked] — [Miller 1956](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Miller/)
- **Miller's own caveat:** absolute judgement and immediate memory are "quite different kinds of limitations"; he suspected the shared seven was "only a pernicious, Pythagorean coincidence". [checked] — [Miller 1956](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Miller/)
- **Cowan (2010), review of his 2001 BBS proposal:** a central store "limited to 3 to 5 meaningful items in young adults"; model fits "settle on a value of about 4". [checked] — [Cowan 2010, Current Directions in Psychological Science](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2864034/)
- **Cowan (2010), conditions:** with rehearsal blocked (Chen & Cowan), people "remembered only about 3 units, no matter whether those were singletons or learned pairs"; running span recovers only the last 3 to 5 digits (under about 2 s of speech); young adults recall only 3 or 4 longer verbal chunks such as idioms or short sentences. [checked] — [Cowan 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2864034/)
- **Cowan (2010), cross-modal and developmental:** about 4 squares when attending to squares only, and still about 4 items in total when attention is split across modalities (Saults & Cowan); capacity about 1.5 at age 7 versus about 3.0 in older children and adults. [checked] — [Cowan 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2864034/)
- **Cowan (2010), why 4:** simulations suggest search is most efficient when groups hold about 3.5 items on average. This is a proposed explanation, not a measurement of people. [checked] — [Cowan 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2864034/)
- **Card, Moran & Newell (1983), Model Human Processor parameters:** pure working memory 3 chunks [range 2.5–4.1 or 4.2; secondary sources disagree on the upper bound]; effective working memory, augmented by long-term memory, 7 [5–9] chunks; decay half-life 7 s [5–226 s] overall, 73 s [73–226 s] for 1 chunk, 7 s [5–34 s] for 3 chunks. [secondary] — [Wikipedia: Human processor model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_processor_model); [CMU course page](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~dccrow/gradcore/core.assignment.html); [PMC4591021 Table 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4591021/table/T1)
- **Halford, Baker, McCredden & Bain (2005), relational complexity:** 30 academics interpreted statistical interactions from bar graphs; accuracy and speed fell significantly from three-way to four-way interactions, and five-way interactions were at chance. Authors: "a structure defined on four variables is at the limit of human processing capacity". Problems were built so participants could not decompose them. [abstract] — [Halford et al. 2005, Psychological Science](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15660854/); [University of Queensland release](https://news.uq.edu.au/2005-02-15-how-much-information-too-much-information)
- **Sweller (1994):** "Working memory can store and process no more than a few discrete items at any given time." [checked] — [Sweller 1994, Learning and Instruction 4, 293–312](https://mrbartonmaths.com/resourcesnew/8.%20Research/Explicit%20Instruction/CLT,%20learning%20difficulty%20and%20instructional%20design.pdf)

### Inferences
- The 7 and the 4 measure different things. Seven (Miller; the Model Human Processor's "effective" 7 [5–9]) includes rehearsal and help from long-term memory. Four (Cowan; the Model Human Processor's "pure" 3 [2.5–4.1]) is what remains when those are blocked. A user in the middle of a task spends attention on the task, so the sober planning figure for new, unchunked things held at once is 3 to 4.
- *Holding* and *relating* differ. Halford's limit counts the variables that must be combined in one decision. A command whose effect depends on a mode, a selection, a world and an argument asks the user to relate four variables; that is at the measured ceiling.
- The decay figures (7 s half-life for 3 chunks) imply that anything the user must carry across an interruption of more than a few seconds should be on the screen, not in the head.
- **Known misreading:** "a menu should have 7 ± 2 items". Miller measured recall of presented lists and judgement along one dimension. A menu stays visible, so choosing from it is recognition, not recall; the working-memory limit does not bound the length of a visible list (visual search and decision time do). The limit binds what is *not* visible: the current mode, pending arguments, the meaning of a state, the path back. This is my inference from the scope of Miller's and Cowan's measurements; I found no single paper that tests menu length against span.
- A design rule of "at most about 4 things per act" matches Cowan's central estimate, but Cowan counts *chunks*. For a practised user, a learnt compound counts once (see question 2); for a newcomer, each of its parts counts.

### Gaps
- I did not reach the original Cowan (2001) BBS article or the Card, Moran & Newell (1983) book; the upper bound of pure capacity (4.1 vs 4.2) is unresolved between secondary sources.
- The commonly quoted individual-differences range of about 2 to 6 chunks in adults is not given in Cowan (2010); not confirmed.
- Baddeley's phonological loop and the word-length effect (span ≈ what can be said in about 2 s) were not fetched; Cowan (2010) mentions only the 2 s running-span figure.
- Kahneman was not searched.

## 2. Chunking and long-term working memory: how experts handle more, and what it requires of the material

### Takeaway
Experts do not have a larger working memory: they recode material into chunks stored in long-term memory (chess masters are estimated to hold about 50,000, range 10,000–100,000) and build retrieval structures that let long-term memory serve as working memory. Both take hundreds to thousands of hours, and both depend on the material having stable, recurring structure: on random chess positions the master's advantage largely disappears.

### Cited Findings
- **Chase & Simon (1973):** three players (master, Class A, beginner); on positions from real games the master recalled far more, but on random positions all players recalled only about 3 or 4 pieces. Pauses of about 2 s or more during reconstruction mark chunk boundaries. Criticism: one player per skill level. [secondary] — [Matuschak notes on Chase & Simon](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/Chase_and_Simon_-_Perception_in_chess); [Brunel replication of the copy task](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/1343/1/Copy-Task-NEW-BJP.pdf)
- **The 2 s chunk boundary** proposed by Chase & Simon was found robust in a later replication. [secondary] — [Brunel copy-task paper](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/1343/1/Copy-Task-NEW-BJP.pdf)
- **Size of an expert chunk store:** Simon & Gilmartin (1973) estimated that chess masters hold between 10,000 and 100,000 chunks, usually quoted as 50,000; Gobet & Simon (1996) report that a simulation plus a human experiment support "the usual estimate". [secondary] — [Gobet & Simon 1996 (PDF)](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/chess/1996-gobet.pdf); [Memory & Cognition record](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03200937)
- **Templates:** frequently used chunks become templates, a schema with a constant core and slots for variable information (Gobet & Simon 1996). [secondary] — [Gobet & Simon 1996](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/chess/1996-gobet.pdf)
- **Sweller (1994) on expert stores:** "Simon and Gilmartin (1973) have estimated that in intellectually complex areas experts have acquired tens of thousands of schemas which are the building blocks of intellectual skill." [checked] — [Sweller 1994, pp. 298–299](https://mrbartonmaths.com/resourcesnew/8.%20Research/Explicit%20Instruction/CLT,%20learning%20difficulty%20and%20instructional%20design.pdf)
- **Sweller (1994) on chunking:** "Schemas effectively increase the amount of information that can be held in working memory by chunking individual elements into a single element"; what counts as an element "is affected by" the learner's knowledge; schemas "can act as elements themselves in higher order schemas". [checked] — [Sweller 1994, pp. 299, 305–306](https://mrbartonmaths.com/resourcesnew/8.%20Research/Explicit%20Instruction/CLT,%20learning%20difficulty%20and%20instructional%20design.pdf)
- **Ericsson, Chase & Faloon (1980), subject SF:** digit span rose from 7 to 79 digits after more than 230 hours of laboratory practice (a later report says over 250 hours and about 80 digits over two years). SF recoded digit groups as running times (3492 → "3 minutes and 49 point 2 seconds"). [secondary; hours differ between sources, and I did not isolate which DTIC report gives which] — [DTIC ADA114634](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA114634.pdf); [DTIC ADA193829](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA193829.pdf)
- **Learned pairs act as one chunk:** arbitrary learned pairs such as "desk-ball" were recalled as units, and capacity in units stayed about 3 whether units were singletons or pairs. [checked] — [Cowan 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2864034/)
- **Ericsson & Kintsch (1995), long-term working memory:** skilled people encode information into long-term memory and retrieve it quickly through retrieval structures and elaborated long-term schemas; domains examined include digit span, memory for menu (dinner) orders, mental multiplication, abacus calculation, chess and text comprehension. [secondary] — [CogSci summary of Ericsson & Kintsch 1995, Psychological Review](https://www.jimdavies.org/summaries/ericsson1995.html)
- **Critique:** Gobet (2000) argues that "retrieval structure" conflates three kinds of memory structure, and that the empirical support for long-term working memory is weaker than claimed. [secondary; seen in a search summary, repository record not opened] — [Brunel repository record](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/handle/2438/807?mode=full)

### Inferences
- Chunks are earned by practice, not granted by design. SF needed hundreds of hours for one domain-specific code; chess chunk stores take years. A tool cannot install chunks; it can only (a) reuse chunks the user already has (natural language, common conventions) and (b) keep its own structure regular, so that one learnt pattern covers many cases.
- Regularity is a precondition. The random-position result shows that chunking fails when configurations do not recur. Every exception, special case or irregular behaviour in a tool is a configuration that will not chunk, and so stays a separate element.
- A composable command set (for example, a small set of verbs that apply uniformly to a small set of object kinds) is the software analogue of a template with slots: the user learns the frame once and fills the slots. This is an inference from template theory, not a tested result for software.
- In a daily-use notebook, only operations that recur often enough to be practised will ever chunk; rare operations should be designed to be recognised, not recalled.

### Gaps
- I did not verify the Chase & Simon first-trial figures for real positions (often quoted as master about 16 pieces vs beginner about 4); recalled only.
- I did not verify Gobet & Simon's later finding that masters keep a small advantage even on random positions; recalled only.
- The often-quoted "about 10 years of deliberate practice" for expertise (Ericsson et al. 1993) was not searched.

## 3. How fast arbitrary command-to-action mappings are learnt and forgotten: practice, ACT-R, forgetting, spacing

### Takeaway
Speed-up with practice is steep early and flattens later (a power law for averaged data; exponential within one person). Forgetting follows a curve whose rate tracks how often and how recently an item was needed; spaced practice works best with gaps of roughly 5–40 % of the interval over which it must be kept. In a controlled study, 10 new hotkeys at once in one 40-minute session was the most that could be learnt at all, and with an ordinary menu only 29 % of trials used the hotkey.

### Cited Findings
- **Power vs exponential law of practice:** Heathcote, Brown & Mewhort (2000) fitted 40 data sets (7,910 learning series, 475 subjects, 24 experiments); the exponential function fitted better than the power function in all unaveraged data sets; the power law arises from averaging across people. Proposed APEX function. [abstract] — [Heathcote et al. 2000, Psychonomic Bulletin & Review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03196105)
- **Anderson & Schooler (1991), rational analysis:** the probability that an item will be needed declines as a power function of time since its last use; for New York Times data a power function accounted for 99 % of the variance. The paper treats the practice law, the spacing effect and retention as reflections of this environmental statistic. [abstract/secondary] — [Anderson & Schooler 1991, Psychological Science 2(6), 396–408](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/j.1467-9280.1991.tb00174.x/); [PDF](https://dyurovsky.github.io/learning-humans-machines/class/13-class/papers/anderson1991.pdf)
- **Ebbinghaus forgetting curve replicated:** Murre & Dros (2015), one subject, about 70 hours of learning and relearning nonsense-syllable lists with savings measured after 20 min, 1 h, 9 h, 1 day, 2 days and 31 days; results "similar to Ebbinghaus' original data", with a probable upward jump at 24 h. Single subject; no independent replication on record. [abstract] — [Murre & Dros 2015, PLOS ONE 10(7): e0120644](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4492928/); [FORRT replication atlas](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1371/journal.pone.0120644/)
- **Spacing, Cepeda et al. (2008):** over 1,350 people learnt facts, reviewed after a gap of up to 3.5 months, and were tested after a further delay of up to 1 year. At any test delay, lengthening the gap first raised then slowly lowered final recall. The optimal gap rose with test delay in absolute terms but fell as a proportion: from about 20–40 % of a 1-week delay to about 5–10 % of a 1-year delay. [abstract] — [Cepeda et al. 2008, Psychological Science 19(11), 1095–1102](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/)
- **Very long-term retention, Bahrick (1984):** 733 people tested on Spanish learnt at school, over 50 years; retention declined exponentially for 3–6 years, then stayed unchanged for up to 30 years before a final decline ("permastore"). Retention was predicted by the level of original training; most subjects rehearsed so little that rehearsal had no measurable effect. The permastore interpretation has been disputed (Neisser 1984). [abstract/secondary] — [Bahrick 1984, Journal of Experimental Psychology: General](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6242406/)
- **Spaced relearning, Bahrick & Phelps (1987):** 50 Spanish–English word pairs tested after 8 years; wider spacing of the original relearning sessions improved retention. [secondary] — [Bahrick & Phelps 1987 (PDF)](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/1987-bahrick.pdf)
- **Hotkeys are paired associates:** Grossman, Dragicevic & Balakrishnan (2007) treat hotkey learning as paired-associate learning and note: "As the number of commands increases, the number of meaningful mappings quickly runs out, and the use of arbitrary mappings seems unavoidable." Learning is "especially difficult when they are arbitrary and when they are not consistent across applications". [checked] — [Grossman et al. 2007, CHI](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/~ravin/papers/chi2007_learninghotkeys.pdf)
- **How many at once:** in their pilot, "initial studies showed that presenting more than 10 items at a time would make it too difficult to learn any hotkeys, and presenting fewer than 10 items at a time would make learning the hotkeys too easy" (one session of about 40 minutes). [checked] — [Grossman et al. 2007](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/~ravin/papers/chi2007_learninghotkeys.pdf)
- **Adoption rates:** main experiment, 14 target commands with Zipfian frequencies (12, 6, 4, 3, 2, 2, 1 per 30 trials, two commands at each), 12 blocks of 60 trials, one 30–60 minute session, between-participants. Hotkey use was 28.9 % with an ordinary menu, 66.6 % with audio feedback, 72.8 % with the menu disabled; frequent commands were significantly more likely to have their hotkeys learnt (p < .0001). The techniques raised hotkey use "by over 100%". Error rates were about 4–6 %. [checked] — [Grossman et al. 2007](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/~ravin/papers/chi2007_learninghotkeys.pdf)
- **Failure to transition:** Lane, Napier, Peres & Sándor (2005) report that experienced users often keep using menus and toolbars instead of shortcuts, even when shortcuts are faster. A second-hand quotation of the paper gives, for Save in Word, toolbar about 50 %, menu about 35 %, Ctrl+S about 7 % (self-report survey). [secondary; the percentages come from a mailing-list post and are unverified] — [Lane et al. 2005, IJHCI 18, 133–144](https://researchr.org/publication/LaneNPS05); [GNOME list quotation](https://lists.gnome.org/archives/desktop-devel-list/2005-October/msg00223.html)

### Inferences
- Retention tracks frequency and recency of use (Anderson & Schooler). A command used daily stays available; a command used monthly falls back toward forgotten between uses. Infrequent operations must therefore be findable by recognition (a searchable list, a menu with the key shown), not only by recall.
- Grossman's pilot gives a rough acquisition ceiling: about 10 new arbitrary mappings in one focused 40-minute session was the edge of learnable. In real use, where learning is incidental, the rate will be lower. This is an estimate drawn from one lab study, not a measured field rate.
- Even after learning, users fall back to menus when menus are equally available (control: 29 % hotkey use). A recall-based vocabulary grows only when the tool makes the recalled path pay, or makes the recognised path teach it.
- Bahrick's data suggest that a core vocabulary practised daily over months (overlearnt, spaced) will survive breaks of weeks or years. Material learnt once and rarely used decays exponentially over the first years.
- Because individual learning curves are steep early, most of the speed-up for a single operation probably arrives in its first few dozen uses; I found no figure for software commands specifically.

### Gaps
- I did not find a measured figure for how many arbitrary paired associates (command names, key bindings) people retain in real software with occasional use. None of the sources gives one.
- Myung, Kim & Pitt (2000) reportedly argue that power functions fit random noise well, and a simulation reportedly shows that averaging individual exponential curves yields an aggregate power curve; both seen only in a search summary, not read. Background: [Wikipedia: Power law of practice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_law_of_practice)
- Newell & Rosenbloom (1981) original exponents and their chunking account were not fetched; recalled only.
- ACT-R's base-level learning equation and its customary decay parameter d = 0.5 were not confirmed by any source I reached; recalled only.
- The Cepeda et al. (2006) spacing meta-analysis (Psychological Bulletin) was not fetched.

## 4. Cognitive load theory: intrinsic and extraneous load, element interactivity, and how many interacting elements a learner can process

### Takeaway
Cognitive load theory separates the *count* of things to learn from their *interactivity*. Independent elements (paired associates, a vocabulary) can be learnt one at a time, so a long list is slow but not overwhelming; interacting elements must be processed together, so they hit the working-memory limit at once. Interactivity created by the presentation, not by the task, is extraneous load and can be designed away. The theory gives no number of its own; the numeric ceiling comes from working memory (about 4; Halford's 4 variables).

### Cited Findings
- **Definition:** "Elements interact if they are related in a manner that requires them to be assimilated simultaneously… In contrast, elements do not interact if they can be assimilated serially." [checked] — [Sweller 1994, p. 304](https://mrbartonmaths.com/resourcesnew/8.%20Research/Explicit%20Instruction/CLT,%20learning%20difficulty%20and%20instructional%20design.pdf)
- **Paired associates as the low extreme:** "Paired associate learning is probably the ultimate in low element interactivity because the paired associates can be learned one at a time"; difficulty "is closely related to the number of items on the list", and such a task "can be very difficult if the list is long enough". Second-language vocabulary is the real-world example. [checked] — [Sweller 1994, p. 304](https://mrbartonmaths.com/resourcesnew/8.%20Research/Explicit%20Instruction/CLT,%20learning%20difficulty%20and%20instructional%20design.pdf)
- **High interactivity examples:** English word order ("when learning English" vs "English when learning"), multiplying out a denominator, moving on both axes of a coordinate system at once. Anatomy terminology is difficult "because of the amount of information, not because of element interactivity". [checked] — [Sweller 1994, pp. 304–305](https://mrbartonmaths.com/resourcesnew/8.%20Research/Explicit%20Instruction/CLT,%20learning%20difficulty%20and%20instructional%20design.pdf)
- **Irreducible interaction:** steps can first be learnt serially, but when they must be combined, "No matter how well automated the individual elements are, at this point they and their relations must be consciously considered simultaneously." [checked] — [Sweller 1994, p. 305](https://mrbartonmaths.com/resourcesnew/8.%20Research/Explicit%20Instruction/CLT,%20learning%20difficulty%20and%20instructional%20design.pdf)
- **Learner-relative elements:** "we cannot determine beforehand, merely by analysing the materials, what constitutes an element"; an expert's whole procedure is one element. "A precise measure of element interactivity that is independent of the learner is unobtainable." [checked] — [Sweller 1994, pp. 305–306](https://mrbartonmaths.com/resourcesnew/8.%20Research/Explicit%20Instruction/CLT,%20learning%20difficulty%20and%20instructional%20design.pdf)
- **Extraneous load:** extraneous cognitive load "is entirely under instructional control". [checked] — [Sweller 1994, p. 303](https://mrbartonmaths.com/resourcesnew/8.%20Research/Explicit%20Instruction/CLT,%20learning%20difficulty%20and%20instructional%20design.pdf)
- **Sweller (2010):** element interactivity also underlies extraneous load; the distinction between intrinsic and extraneous load depends on whether the interactivity is essential to the task or a product of the instructional procedure; germane load is redefined in terms of intrinsic load. [abstract] — [Sweller 2010, Educational Psychology Review 22, 123–138](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10648-010-9128-5)
- **Status and debate:** a 2023 review calls element interactivity a cornerstone that determines all cognitive load effects; critics dispute whether intrinsic load is fixed or can be altered by instruction. [secondary] — [Educational Psychology Review 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-023-09782-w)
- **Numeric ceiling for interacting variables:** four-way interactions degrade performance significantly; five-way at chance (Halford et al. 2005). [abstract] — [PubMed 15660854](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15660854/)
- **Reducing functionality helps novices:** Carroll & Carrithers' "Training Wheels" word processor blocked functions not needed for simple tasks; novices finished tasks significantly faster and with significantly fewer errors than with the full version. [secondary, via McGrenere & Moore] — [McGrenere & Moore 2000, §2](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/~joanna/papers/gi_2000_bloat.pdf)

### Inferences
- Two budgets follow, and they must not be confused. (a) A **vocabulary budget**: the number of independent operations, learnt serially over weeks; cost grows with count and with interference (question 5), not with working memory. (b) An **interaction budget**: the number of elements that must be combined in one act (mode, selection, argument, scope, world); bounded near 4 by working memory, and paid on every use until it chunks.
- An operation whose meaning depends on other state (modes, context-sensitive keys) converts a low-interactivity vocabulary item into a high-interactivity one. Where that dependence is not essential to the user's task, it is extraneous load by Sweller's 2010 definition, and the designer can remove it.
- Composition is cheap only if the parts are regular: a verb that means the same thing on every object kind is learnt once (serially) and combined without new interaction; a verb whose meaning varies by object kind adds an interacting element for each variant.
- The Training Wheels result supports staging: a small initial set, with the rest revealed as needed, lowers novice errors.

### Gaps
- The often-quoted statement that people can *process* only "two or three" novel elements at once (Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas 1998, Educational Psychology Review 10, 251–296) could not be verified; the full text was not reachable. Recalled only. [Record](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/cognitive-architecture-and-instructional-design/)
- No source gave a validated method for counting element interactivity in a software interface.

## 5. Proactive and retroactive interference: why similar or colliding names cost more than their count

### Takeaway
Forgetting is driven more by competing learnt material than by time: next-day recall fell from about 80 % with no prior lists to about 20 % after 20 prior lists. Each extra fact attached to one cue slows retrieval of all of them (the fan effect). People rarely agree on names (under 20 % of the time), so a name chosen by the designer will often collide with the user's own word for something else.

### Cited Findings
- **Underwood (1957):** pooling earlier studies, recall of a list a day later fell from about .80 with no preceding lists to about .20 after about 20 preceding lists; Underwood argued that much forgetting attributed to decay is proactive interference, and that proactive inhibition matters more than retroactive inhibition. [secondary; exact start figure varies between 70–80 % in accounts] — [Memory & Cognition 2010 article citing Underwood](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-010-0017-4); [Simply Psychology](https://www.simplypsychology.org/proactive-and-retroactive-interference.html); [Wikipedia: Interference theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interference_theory)
- **Fan effect (Anderson 1974):** recognition time increases with the number of facts associated with a concept (its "fan"); in the original, participants learnt 26 person–location sentences. The effect extends to real-world knowledge (Lewis & Anderson 1976), faces (Anderson & Paulson 1978) and alphabet-arithmetic facts. Usual account: activation from a cue is divided among its associations, so each is weaker, or the associations compete. [secondary] — [Wikipedia: Fan effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fan_effect)
- **Naming disagreement, Furnas, Landauer, Gomez & Dumais (1987):** across five application domains, two people favoured the same term with probability under 0.20; reliance on one designer-chosen word gives 80–90 % failure in many common situations; "unlimited aliasing" gave several-fold improvement. [abstract/secondary] — [Furnas et al. 1987, CACM 30(11), 964–971](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/furnas-landauer-gomez-dumais-thevocabularyprobleminhumansystemcommunication-1987); [dblp](https://dblp1.uni-trier.de/rec/journals/cacm/FurnasLGD87.html)
- **Re-association avoided by experimenters:** Grossman et al. chose a between-participants design partly because "it would be confusing if participants were forced to memorize new associations for the same accelerator keys". Inconsistency of hotkeys across applications makes them harder to learn. [checked] — [Grossman et al. 2007](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/~ravin/papers/chi2007_learninghotkeys.pdf)
- **Even independent pairs interfere:** "While there may be some unintended interference between paired-associates, each pair can be learned in isolation." [checked] — [Sweller 1994, p. 304](https://mrbartonmaths.com/resourcesnew/8.%20Research/Explicit%20Instruction/CLT,%20learning%20difficulty%20and%20instructional%20design.pdf)

### Inferences
- One word with two meanings in a tool is the classic A–B, A–C arrangement: one cue, two responses. It produces proactive interference (the old meaning intrudes) and retroactive interference (the new meaning weakens the old), and by the fan effect it slows retrieval of *both* meanings on every use. Its cost is therefore larger than one extra vocabulary item, and it does not fall to zero with practice in the way an independent item's cost does. (Inference from the paradigm; I found no software study that measures it.)
- The same key bound to different actions in different modes is the same arrangement, plus an interacting element (the mode) that must be held.
- Many words for one concept (aliases) is the reverse mapping: many cues, one response. Furnas shows it aids finding. Whether it adds interference is not settled by these sources; my expectation is that it adds little, since all cues lead to the same response, but this is untested here.
- Underwood's figures mean that each additional, similar command learnt makes the earlier ones harder to recall, so a vocabulary's cost grows faster than its count when its items resemble each other.

### Gaps
- The similarity gradient of interference (Osgood 1949 transfer surface; McGeoch) was not fetched.
- A report that the fan effect vanished after massive practice (Hayes-Roth 1977, on day 10) appeared in a search summary; I could not tie it to a page I read, so it is unverified.
- No study found that measures the cost of homonymous command names in software directly.

## 6. Measured or defensible size of the vocabulary of operations one person commands fluently in one tool

### Takeaway
In a mature application with 265 first-level functions (Word 97), 53 users reported using on average 72 (27 %) and using 40 (15 %) regularly, while recognising 135 (51 %); the heaviest user used 119. Logging studies of Unix and word processors agree that a small core of commands carries most use. Expert domains with years of practice support tens of thousands of chunks, so long-term memory is not the binding limit; practice frequency is.

### Cited Findings
- **McGrenere & Moore (2000), Word 97, n = 53 (self-report):** 265 first-level functions. Mean familiar 135 (51 %), mean used 72 (27 %), used regularly 40 (15 %), used irregularly 32 (12 %). Range familiar 24–245 (9–92 %); range used 8–119 (3–45 %). Usage-to-familiarity ratio 57 % (SD 0.148). 42 functions (15.8 %) were used by no one; 57 (21.5 %) were used by more than half; only 12 were used regularly by more than three-quarters (the paper prints 3.3 %, but 12/265 is 4.5 %, so one of the two figures is a misprint). [checked] — [McGrenere & Moore 2000, Graphics Interface, Table 1 and §4](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/~joanna/papers/gi_2000_bloat.pdf)
- **The "80/20" summary:** logging studies "consistently show that users of complex software use very few of the commands available the majority of the time; informally this is called the 80/20 rule". [checked] — [McGrenere & Moore 2000, §2](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/~joanna/papers/gi_2000_bloat.pdf)
- **Linton et al. (OWL logger):** the paper cites "one study" showing users apply less than 10 % of Microsoft Word's commands. [abstract/secondary] — [Linton et al., AAAI-98 workshop paper](https://www.aaai.org/Papers/Workshops/1998/WS-98-08/WS98-08-017.pdf)
- **Microsoft telemetry, Word 2003:** five commands accounted for about 32 % of all command use. [secondary, vendor blog] — [Jensen Harris, "No Distaste for Paste"](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/archive/blogs/jensenh/no-distaste-for-paste-why-the-ui-part-7)
- **Unix shell:** R. Hanson (1986, Berkeley) reports that the average C-shell user uses about one-fifth of all commands, and most command lines contain one command. Hanson, Kraut & Farber (1984) logged 170 users (process accounting) and 16 users (command lines) and found a modular core of commands plus fixed chains. Greenberg called systems where users reuse a small subset of the repertoire "recurrent systems". [abstract/secondary] — [Berkeley technical report record](https://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/1986/6101.html); [Hanson, Kraut & Farber 1984, ACM TOIS 2(1), 42–57](https://researchr.org/publication/HansonKF84); [Greenberg 1993, "Using commands in UNIX"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/computer-user-as-toolsmith/using-commands-in-unix/4CA1BDB47ED1CE7E7A4012E4AA9BB678); [McGrenere & Moore 2000, §2](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/~joanna/papers/gi_2000_bloat.pdf)
- **Command use is Zipfian:** Grossman et al. used a Zipfian distribution "which has been shown to represent command use frequency in real applications". [checked] — [Grossman et al. 2007](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/~ravin/papers/chi2007_learninghotkeys.pdf)
- **Expert ceiling:** chess masters about 50,000 chunks (range 10,000–100,000); "tens of thousands of schemas" in intellectually complex areas. [secondary/checked] — [Gobet & Simon 1996](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/chess/1996-gobet.pdf); [Sweller 1994, p. 299](https://mrbartonmaths.com/resourcesnew/8.%20Research/Explicit%20Instruction/CLT,%20learning%20difficulty%20and%20instructional%20design.pdf)

### Inferences
- The best measured anchor for "operations a person keeps using" in a daily general-purpose tool is about 40 used regularly and about 70 used at all, out of 265, with menus present. The spread is wide (8 to 119 used). These are self-reports from 2000, not logs; logging studies give lower fractions (under 10 %).
- Recognition and recall vocabularies differ by a large factor: people recognised about twice as many functions as they used (ratio 57 %), and the Word figures were measured with menus and toolbars available. A recall-only vocabulary (typed commands, hotkeys) is smaller still; the Grossman and Lane data show that users do not move most operations to recall even when it is faster.
- Defensible planning range for a single researcher's daily notebook (an estimate built from the above, not a measurement): a recalled core of a few dozen operations used daily or weekly, each regular in form; a recognised outer set of perhaps a hundred or more, reachable through a visible, searchable list; and no more than about 4 interacting elements in any one act. Growth beyond the core should come by composition of already-known parts, not by new arbitrary names.
- The ceiling on vocabulary is set by practice frequency, not storage: Zipfian use means most of a large command set is used too rarely to be practised, and so stays unlearnt (Anderson & Schooler; Grossman's frequency effect).

### Gaps
- I found no study giving a measured count of the keyboard shortcuts typical users know or use; Lane et al.'s figures reached me only second-hand.
- No data found on command repertoires of heavy Emacs or Vim users, on sign or notation systems, or on natural-language vocabulary size as an upper bound (Brysbaert et al. 2016, about 42,000 lemmas for a typical adult, is recalled only and was not fetched).
- The logging figures behind "fewer than 10 %" (Linton) and "one-fifth" (Hanson 1986) were not read in full.
